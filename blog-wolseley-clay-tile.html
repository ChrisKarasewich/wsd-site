Why Wolseley clay tile keeps failing at the 60-foot mark.

A pattern we keep seeing on the south side: clay tile joints letting in roots right where the line crosses the boulevard. Here is what the camera shows — and why spot repair beats a full dig.

JR
J. Reimer
Lead tech · Winnipeg Sewer & Drain

We've run a camera down so many Wolseley laterals this spring that we started flagging the footage on a map. The pattern is hard to miss: the failure point keeps showing up between 55 and 65 feet from the cleanout — which is, almost without exception, the moment the line crosses the boulevard and hits the city main.

That's not a coincidence. The boulevard is where a 1920s house's clay tile lateral meets soil that's been dug up and back-filled half a dozen times over the last century — water main work, hydro trenches, the new fibre run last summer. Every dig is another joint stressed, another inch of bedding lost, another invitation for the roots from the boulevard elms to find the joint and walk in.

What the camera actually shows

Clay tile fails predictably. It doesn't shear in the middle of a length — that part of the pipe is fine, sometimes for a hundred years. It fails at the joints, where two bell-and-spigot sections meet. A small root finds the gap, gets a drink, and over a few seasons it grows from a hair to a rope to a curtain.

On the camera, a 60-foot Wolseley failure looks the same almost every time:

  • 50–55 ft: Pipe is clean. Maybe a faint root hair at one joint. Nothing to act on.
  • 55–60 ft: First curtain root mass — usually on the upstream side of a joint. Looks like a beard hanging into the flow.
  • 60–62 ft: The serious one. A heavy root mass narrowing the line to maybe 40% of its diameter. This is what's actually backing up the basement.
  • 62–70 ft: Pipe widens back out into the city main. Often a small offset where the bell joint dropped a few millimetres.
What that means for you
If a Wolseley house gets a recurring basement backup every spring or fall, it's almost always the boulevard joint. Cabling the line gets you through the year. Spot-repairing that one joint gets you through the decade.

Why spot repair beats a full dig

The instinct — and what the older shops still recommend — is to dig the lateral out from the house to the main. Twenty thousand dollars, two days of yard torn up, three more days for the landscaping. We almost never recommend it.

A spot repair is what it sounds like. We locate the bad joint on the camera, pothole down at exactly that spot, cut out the bad two feet of clay, sleeve in a PVC repair section, and close it back up. One small hole in the yard, one day, a fraction of the cost. The rest of the clay tile — which has held up for a hundred years — keeps doing its job.

The only time a full dig makes sense is when the camera shows multiple failure points along the run, or when the pipe is so soft we can hear it crumble on the cable. Out of about 40 Wolseley laterals we cameraed last spring, three of them needed the full job. The other 37 were spot fixes.

If you're on a Wolseley block

We'd rather camera before there's a backup than after. A pre-emptive inspection is free if we end up doing the work, and a flat fee if we don't. The footage tells you exactly what shape your lateral is in, and gives you a baseline to compare against in two years when you're wondering whether that slow tub is a hair clog or the start of a root job.

Book one through the contact page or call the dispatcher. Mention the street — we'll batch the camera runs when we can, which is how we noticed the pattern in the first place.
